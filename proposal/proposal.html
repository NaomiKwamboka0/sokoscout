Investment proposal

Should you sell in Kenya?

Every week, vendors across the world commit stock to Kenyan e-commerce without knowing what anything sells for, who they are competing against, or which platform will actually move their product. SokoScout answers those questions from real marketplace data, on a phone, for the price of a cup of coffee.

01

The problem

A vendor deciding whether to enter Kenyan e-commerce is making a stock purchase decision with almost no information. The decision is expensive, it is made in the dark, and it is made constantly.

Picture a trading company in Guangzhou with a container of wireless earbuds. They know Kenya is a growing market. They do not know what an earbud sells for in Nairobi, how many sellers already list one, whether the category is saturated, what Jumia takes in commission, what last mile delivery costs, or whether the buyer will pay on delivery in cash. So they guess.

The same is true of a Turkish homeware exporter, a Nigerian phone accessory distributor, and a Kenyan in the diaspora who wants to start selling back home. They are all standing at the same window with the same question, and none of them can see through it.

What they do instead

Today the research options are all bad:

  • Browse the marketplaces by hand. Open Jumia, search a keyword, eyeball ten prices. This gives a feeling, not a number, and it captures one moment rather than a trend. It also cannot count sellers or measure saturation.
  • Ask in a group chat. Anecdote from whoever answers, weighted by how confident they sound.
  • Buy a market report. Expensive, generic, usually a year old, and written at country level rather than at the level of the actual product they intend to ship.
  • Hire a local consultant. Works, and costs more than the first shipment's margin.
  • Ship and find out. The most common option, and the reason so much stock sits unsold in Nairobi warehouses.

Why the cost is high

These are not idle questions. Getting them wrong has three specific expensive outcomes, and each one is common enough that anybody in Kenyan e-commerce can name examples.

FailureWhat actually happensCost to the vendor
Priced wrong Lands at a price the market will not pay, because the reference price used was a European or Nigerian one. Sits unsold, then gets discounted below cost to clear. Margin gone, sometimes the whole consignment
Entered a saturated category Picks a category that looks large because there is a lot of it, when volume is a symptom of two hundred sellers already fighting over the same buyer. Immediate price war, no route to a margin
Wrong platform Lists on a marketplace when the product actually moves through on demand delivery, or ships to a county where the platform has no collection point, so the listing never converts. Months lost, then a second setup cost

The gap in one sentence. The data needed to answer these questions is already public and already sitting on the marketplaces. Nobody has assembled it into something a vendor can simply ask a question of.

02

The insight, and why this is buildable now

Three things became true recently, and together they make a product that would not have worked two years ago.

One. The answer is already published

Kenyan marketplaces publish their catalogues to be found. Jumia Kenya maintains public sitemaps precisely so that search engines can index its products, and its own robots file explicitly permits crawling product pages, specifications, and ratings. Commission schedules and seller terms are published documents. This is not private data and it does not need to be obtained by trickery. It needs to be collected, structured, and kept over time.

Two. Time series is the part nobody has

A single snapshot of prices is mildly useful. A record of what a category's prices did over the last two quarters is a different product: it shows direction, seasonality, and whether new entrants are pushing prices down. Nobody can acquire that retroactively. It only exists if somebody started collecting it, which means the value of the asset compounds from the day the crawler first runs.

Three. Asking in plain language finally became practical to build

Structured market data has always been unusable by the people who need it most, because using it required knowing how to query a database. A vendor does not want a dashboard with forty filters. They want to ask a question in their own words and get an answer. Retrieval techniques and small open weights models that run on ordinary hardware made that interaction buildable, and affordable to operate, only recently. It is what turns a database into a product.

The trap we deliberately avoid. The obvious build is to feed scraped pages to a language model and let it answer. That produces confident, plausible, wrong numbers, because language models do not do arithmetic reliably and will happily invent an average. Our architecture never asks the model to compute anything. The database computes every number; the model chooses which question to ask of it and explains the result. This is covered in section 5 and it is the single most important engineering decision in the product.

Why Kenya, and why now

Kenya is the right first market rather than an arbitrary one:

  • It is the market that is winning. Glovo opened a Nairobi headquarters in 2026 and committed a further KES 10 billion to Kenya through 2030, describing Kenya as by a clear margin its strongest African market. That is a platform voting with its balance sheet.
  • Consolidation makes platform choice matter more. Uber withdrew from Nigeria and Uganda in 2026 while holding Kenya. When platforms are entering and exiting, the question of which one to build on stops being academic.
  • Mobile money removes the payment excuse. A 200 shilling subscription is collectable from a Kenyan seller by M Pesa and from a foreign vendor by card, through one integration.
  • The buyer is mobile only. So is the vendor we are selling to. A phone first product is the correct product, not a compromise.

Sources: Glovo Kenya headquarters and investment commitment, tech-ish.com, May 2026. Uber market withdrawals from Nigeria and Uganda, Techweez and TechCabal, September 2026. Jumia Kenya robots and sitemap policy, retrieved 9 September 2026.

03

The product

A vendor signs up, picks where in Kenya they care about, asks a question in their own words, and gets a number with its evidence attached. Four screens, built for a phone.

Note the fourth card on the comparison screen. Uber Eats is labelled as manual data and Kilimall is labelled as category level rather than product level. We show our own coverage limits inside the product. A vendor about to spend money can tell the difference between a number from twelve hundred observations and a number from forty, and hiding that distinction is how a tool like this loses trust permanently.

Location is a first class input

Every question is answered against a place, because Kenyan commerce is not uniform. A vendor can ask about all of Kenya, narrow to one of fourteen counties with confirmed platform logistics, or narrow again to a town or ward: Gikomba and the CBD plazas for the fashion and thrift trade, Nyali or Kongowea for the coast, Kitengela and Ongata Rongai for the Nairobi commuter belt. The county list is not free text. It is drawn from a maintained coverage file, so a vendor cannot be shown an encouraging answer about a county where the platform cannot actually collect or deliver.

04

How it works

Collect nightly, aggregate into a catalogue, answer questions from the catalogue. No user question ever triggers a live scrape.

What we can actually see, honestly

We verified each platform against its own published crawling policy and its live behaviour on 9 September 2026. The results are uneven, and any proposal that claimed otherwise would be misleading.

PlatformAccessWhat their own policy and behaviour permitsOur approach
Jumia Kenya Good Robots file permits product pages, specifications and ratings. Two public sitemaps. Facets, keyword search and seller pages disallowed. Primary source. Sitemap driven. Product level prices and seller counts.
Kilimall Kenya Partial Robots file explicitly disallows product and item pages and the internal API. Sitemap and category pages are permitted. Category level only. Coarser price data, and labelled as such in the product.
Glovo Kenya Permitted, hard Robots file allows crawling broadly. But listings are rendered in the browser and gated behind entering a delivery address. Structured data payloads behind the rendered page. Higher engineering effort, worth it because Kenya is Glovo's strongest African market.
Uber Eats Blocked Refuses non browser requests outright. Actively bot protected. We do not scrape it. A small, dated, manually maintained fact table, labelled manual everywhere it appears.

Why we are telling you this rather than burying it. Two reasons, both commercial. First, a data product whose collection method is legally fragile is not investable, because the asset can vanish in a single letter. Ours cannot: we read only what these companies publish for crawling, at rates below their thresholds, identifying ourselves honestly. Second, the coverage gaps are a roadmap, not an embarrassment. They tell you exactly what a data partnership or a modest data budget would buy.

The collection discipline we inherited

The prototype this is built from taught three lessons the hard way, and each is now a rule in the codebase:

  • An empty result must raise an error, not pass silently. Anti bot systems signal throttling by returning nothing successfully rather than by returning an error. A collector without this check looks perfectly healthy while collecting zero rows. This is the failure most likely to cost a week.
  • Reruns must resume, not restart. A long crawl will be interrupted. Raw records are unique on platform, product and date, and the crawler checkpoints after each unit, so a rerun continues where it stopped.
  • Run it against the real site before building on top of it. On the predecessor's first live run, the results included sellers in Cape Town and Indonesia, and a national newspaper scored as a top retailer. Every one of those defects was invisible in design and obvious within one real run.
05

The answer engine, and why it can be trusted

This is the part of the product that could most easily be built badly, and the part a technical investor should interrogate hardest. So here is exactly how it works.

The questions split in two, and so does the architecture

Look at what vendors actually ask:

Arithmetic questions

  • What is the median price of a wireless earbud on Jumia Kenya?
  • How many distinct sellers list one?
  • Which category is least saturated right now?
  • What has this category's price done since June?

These are database aggregates. They have exactly one right answer, and it is computable.

Qualitative questions

  • What commission does this category attract?
  • What are the seller onboarding requirements?
  • Can this platform deliver to Kisumu?
  • How does pay on delivery affect my cash cycle?

These are answered from documents: published commission schedules, seller terms, logistics coverage.

The naive build treats both as a retrieval problem, embeds everything, and asks a language model to read the retrieved chunks and produce a number. That fails on the left hand column, and it fails in the worst possible way: fluently. The model returns a confident average that is not the average of anything.

So we split it, and no model ever computes a number. A question is classified, then routed. Arithmetic questions run a typed, parameterised SQL query over the catalogue: category, platform, county, window. Postgres computes the median, the seller count, the trend. The figures come back and are placed into an answer by template, so the number in the sentence is the number the database returned, every time, with no opportunity for anything to invent one. Qualitative questions retrieve the relevant passages from the document corpus and answer from those passages, with the passage cited.

Retrieval, and the generation step, entirely on our own infrastructure

There is no external language model API anywhere in this product. That is a deliberate architectural decision with three separate payoffs.

LayerWhat it isWhy this way
Question routing Rule and pattern based classification into an intent: price level, seller count, saturation, trend, margin, policy, coverage. The same deterministic technique already proven in the predecessor's product classifier. An intent that is wrong is fixed by editing one line, and the same question always routes the same way.
Numeric retrieval A fixed library of parameterised SQL queries over the catalogue and its nightly rollups. Parameters validated against enumerations, never free text. Nothing can write ad hoc SQL, so nothing can write damaging or wrong SQL.
Document retrieval Postgres full text search over the corpus of commission schedules, seller terms, category definitions and logistics coverage, with local sentence embeddings for semantic recall where keyword search is too literal. The embedding model runs on our own CPU, is about ninety megabytes, and costs nothing per query.
Answer assembly Templates for every numeric intent. A self hosted open weights instruct model, running on our own machine, only for phrasing open ended questions and for stitching a multi part answer together from retrieved facts. Fixed machine cost, no metered bill, and the model is never the source of a figure.

The three payoffs, in order of commercial weight:

  • Marginal cost per answered question is effectively zero. This is what makes a 200 shilling subscription genuinely profitable rather than marginally so, and it is why our gross margin does not degrade as usage grows. Inference is a fixed line in the cost table, not a variable one.
  • No dependency on a metered third party. A product whose core function is billed per call by an external provider has somebody else's hand on its cost base and its availability. Ours does not.
  • The questions never leave our infrastructure. What a vendor asks reveals exactly what they are about to invest in. That is among the most commercially sensitive things a business has, and we can tell a customer truthfully that it is never transmitted to a third party model provider. For the agency and brand tiers this is a selling point rather than a footnote.

Every number carries its evidence

No answer is allowed to state a figure without stating what it rests on. Each numeric claim is returned with the number of observations behind it, which platforms they came from, and when they were collected. Below a minimum observation threshold the product declines to answer and says the data is too thin, rather than producing a number from eleven listings and letting a vendor treat it as a market rate.

Because the numbers come from templates over query results rather than from generated text, this is not a behaviour we are hoping for. The citation is assembled from the same query that produced the figure, so a figure without its evidence is not a thing the system can emit.

What we give up, stated plainly. A frontier hosted model would handle a rambling, badly phrased, multi part question more gracefully than our routing will, and would write more elegant prose. We accept that. In exchange we get answers that are reproducible, auditable, free at the margin, and private. For a product whose entire value is that a vendor can trust the number, that is the correct trade. Where the routing genuinely cannot place a question, the product says so and offers the nearest thing it can answer, which is a better failure than a fluent guess.

It is below the threshold at which a vendor stops to think. It is collectable by mobile money without friction. The mitigation is unglamorous and it is in the plan: queue depth and answer latency are monitored from day one, quotas exist as a fairness valve, and numeric answers, which are the majority, never touch the constrained resource at all.

08

What makes this defensible

The scraper is not the moat. Anybody can write a scraper. Three other things are much harder to copy.

TimePrice history cannot be acquired retroactively
LanguageA classifier tuned to Kenyan retail vocabulary
QuestionsA corpus of what vendors actually want to know
TrustCited, refusable answers rather than confident guesses

One. The time series compounds from day one

A competitor starting in six months can crawl today's prices, and they will match our snapshot within a week. What they cannot do is obtain what a category's prices did last quarter. That record only exists because somebody was collecting it, and its value grows every night the collector runs. Trend, seasonality and the price impact of new entrants are the answers vendors most want, and they are precisely the answers that require history.

Two. Kenyan retail vocabulary is not generic

Classifying what a listing actually is, in a market where the same product is described as a power bank, a powerbank, a phone charger and a portable charger, and where a seller writes prices as 1.5k, is a solved problem in our codebase and an unsolved one in anybody else's. The predecessor project built and tested this against real Kenyan listings, deliberately as explicit rules rather than as a model, so that a wrong classification is fixed by editing one line and the same input always produces the same output. That determinism is why the numbers are reproducible, and reproducibility is what a vendor is paying for.

Three. The question log is the product roadmap

Every question asked is recorded to a reviewed Google Sheet. Reviewers mark whether the answer was right and correct it where it was not. That produces three assets in order of value:

  1. A gap list. Questions vendors ask that our catalogue cannot answer. This is the highest value input to what we collect next, and no amount of engineering intuition substitutes for it.
  2. An evaluation set. Real questions with verified answers, so a change to the engine can be proven not to have made things worse.
  3. Few shot examples for question shapes that route to the wrong tool.

We are deliberately not claiming this trains a custom model. Fine tuning is not the near term play and any proposal that promises it is overselling. What the log genuinely buys is knowing what to build next, and being able to prove the product is improving. That is worth more at this stage than a fine tuned model would be.

Four. The honesty is a feature competitors will struggle to copy

A competitor built on a language model reading scraped pages will produce answers that look better than ours, because they will always produce an answer. Ours will sometimes say the data is too thin. The first time a vendor commits stock on a confident hallucinated number and loses money, that competitor is finished in this market. Refusing to guess is a slower path to a demo and a faster path to a business.

09

Security, privacy and compliance

This product holds foreign vendors' identities, payment references, and their commercial strategy. What a vendor asks reveals what they are about to invest in, which is among the most sensitive things a business has. That shapes the architecture.

The predecessor's cautionary tale

The prototype this is built from has no authentication whatsoever. Every route on its public URL is open: any visitor can trigger a data collection run, read every scraped phone number, and download the entire database as a spreadsheet. It was written to run on a laptop, then deployed to the internet, and the assumption did not survive the move. We are naming this because it is the clearest possible statement of what this build does differently and why authentication is phase one rather than phase four.

How access works

  • Passwords hashed with argon2id, the current recommendation, not a general purpose hash.
  • Server held sessions in a cookie marked HttpOnly, Secure and SameSite, rather than a self contained token. The reason is specific: a subscription that lapses or a payment that fails must revoke access immediately, and a self contained token cannot be revoked before it expires without keeping a server side list anyway.
  • Email verified before first sign in, single use time limited reset links, and every existing session invalidated when a password changes.
  • Rate limiting and progressive lockout on authentication endpoints, with identical responses whether or not an account exists, so the login form cannot be used to enumerate customers.
  • Tenant isolation at the query layer. Every read is filtered by account in SQL rather than in application logic, so there is no code path that can return another vendor's data.

Threats specific to this kind of product

ThreatWhy it matters hereMitigation
Capacity abuse Answers cost us nothing per question, so this is not a financial attack. It is a denial of service one: a scripted trial account can occupy the inference machine and make every paying subscriber's answers slow. Per account quota and per account queue fairness, checked before work is admitted. Numeric answers bypass the constrained resource entirely, so the majority of traffic is unaffected even under load.
Prompt injection through scraped content We ingest text written by third parties. A crafted product title could try to issue instructions to the phrasing model. Scraped text never enters a system prompt. The model receives typed numeric results and labelled snippets, never raw pages, and it never has a tool it could be induced to call.
Injection through the query layer Anything that composes SQL from user input is a place SQL injection lives. No component writes ad hoc SQL. The router selects from a fixed library of statements and supplies parameters validated against enumerations. Every statement is parameterised.
Forged payment webhooks A spoofed renewal event grants a free subscription. Signature verified before the payload is parsed, and processing is idempotent because payment providers retry.
Strategy leakage A vendor's question history is competitively valuable and must not leak, including into our own analytics. The question log carries a pseudonymous account reference, never the email address.

Data protection posture

Kenya's Data Protection Act 2019 governs personal data regardless of where it was found, and a sole trader's contact details are personal data. Our position, carried forward from the predecessor and tightened:

  • We collect product and price data, which is commercial rather than personal. We deliberately do not build a database of individual sellers' contact details for this product, because it is not needed to answer a vendor's question and it would materially raise our obligations.
  • Nothing behind a login is ever collected. No accounts on the platforms we observe, no automation of logged in sessions.
  • Each platform's published crawling policy is honoured per the table in section 4, including declining to crawl Uber Eats at all.
  • Our own subscribers' data is subject access and deletion capable from day one, and registration with the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner is a launch item rather than an afterthought.

No external model API, so no per question cost and no third party sees a customer's question.
Front endHand written CSS and standard JavaScript modules, no framework and no build step. This is a deliberate operational choice: the predecessor still runs years later partly because there is nothing to rebuild.
HostingFly.io, Johannesburg region, which is the closest low latency region to Kenyan users. Collector on a separate scheduled machine so it can never block a request.
PaymentsPaystack, covering M Pesa and international cards through one integration.
Question logGoogle Sheets through an existing external automation webhook, so no Google credential is ever stored on our servers.
